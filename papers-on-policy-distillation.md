## [2023] On-Policy Distillation of Language Models: Learning from Self-Generated Mistakes

- **Date**: 2026-06-03
- **Arxiv**: <https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13649>

---

- **Abstract**:
  - > Knowledge distillation (KD) is widely used for compressing a teacher model to reduce its inference cost and memory footprint, by training a smaller student model. However, current KD methods for auto-regressive sequence models suffer from distribution mismatch between output sequences seen during training and those generated by the student during inference. To address this issue, we introduce Generalized Knowledge Distillation (GKD). Instead of solely relying on a fixed set of output sequences, GKD trains the student on its self-generated output sequences by leveraging feedback from the teacher on such sequences. Unlike supervised KD approaches, GKD also offers the flexibility to employ alternative loss functions between the student and teacher, which can be useful when the student lacks the expressivity to mimic the teacher's distribution. Furthermore, GKD facilitates the seamless integration of distillation with RL fine-tuning (RLHF). We demonstrate the efficacy of GKD for distilling auto-regressive language models on summarization, translation, and arithmetic reasoning tasks, and task-agnostic distillation for instruction-tuning.
- **Section 1: Introduction**:
  - Motivation: Large autoregressive LMs are capable but expensive to deploy. Distillation is useful if a smaller student can retain much of a larger teacher's performance with lower inference cost and memory footprint.
  - Existing KD methods for autoregressive sequence models:
    - **SeqKD**: generate a fixed set of teacher outputs, then train the student on those sequences. Expensive because it requires teacher generation.
    - **Supervised/token-level KD**: use a fixed dataset of sequences and ask the teacher for token-level probabilities. Cheaper than generating all outputs, but the prefixes are fixed/off-policy.
  - Main issue: distribution mismatch.
    - Training uses teacher/data prefixes.
    - Inference uses student-generated prefixes.
    - If the student makes an early mistake, it conditions on states that were not covered during training; errors can compound.
    - This is the same imitation learning problem as behavior cloning on expert states.
  - Second issue: forward KL under capacity mismatch.
    - Standard distillation often minimizes $\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{teacher}} \Vert p_{\text{student}})$.
    - Forward KL is mode-covering: it pressures the student to cover the teacher's full distribution.
    - If the student is too small to match the teacher, this can lead to bad compromise generations that the teacher itself would not likely produce.
  - Proposed fix: **Generalized Knowledge Distillation (GKD)**.
    - View autoregressive KD as imitation learning with an interactive expert.
    - Let the student generate its own on-policy output sequences.
    - Query the teacher for token-level probabilities on those student-generated prefixes.
    - Train the student using those teacher probabilities as expert labels.
  - GKD separates two knobs:
    - **Data distribution**: fixed/off-policy data vs mixed vs student-generated on-policy data.
    - **Divergence**: forward KL, reverse KL, generalized JSD, etc.
  - Why alternative divergences matter:
    - Reverse KL / JSD can be more mode-seeking.
    - This can help a limited-capacity student focus on samples likely under the teacher rather than covering every teacher mode.
    - Optimal divergence is task-dependent.
  - Claimed results:
    - On-policy GKD improves over baseline KD methods on summarization, machine translation, arithmetic reasoning, and task-agnostic instruction-tuning.
    - They also claim GKD can combine naturally with RL fine-tuning / RLHF-style workflows.
- **Section 2: Preliminaries**:
  - Autoregressive LM setup:
    - Input sequence: $x$.
    - Output sequence: $y = (y_1, \dots, y_{L_y})$.
    - Vocabulary: $\mathbb{V}$ with $M$ tokens.
    - Policy: $p(\cdot \mid y_{<n}, x) \in (0,1)^M$, a next-token distribution conditioned on the prompt and generated prefix.
    - They abbreviate $p(y_n \mid y_{<n}, x)$ as $p(y_n \mid x)$ when the prefix is clear.
  - Temperature sampling:
    - Next-token probabilities come from softmax over logits with temperature $\gamma$.
    - Higher $\gamma$ increases randomness/diversity.
    - Lower $\gamma$ is more deterministic; $\gamma \to 0$ becomes greedy decoding.
    - Student training temperature is kept at 1; evaluation uses greedy or temperature sampling.
  - KL directions:
    - $\mathrm{KL}(P \Vert Q) = \sum_c P(c) \log \frac{P(c)}{Q(c)}$.
    - KL is asymmetric: $\mathrm{KL}(P \Vert Q) \neq \mathrm{KL}(Q \Vert P)$.
    - Forward KL: $\mathrm{KL}(P \Vert Q)$.
    - Reverse KL: $\mathrm{KL}(Q \Vert P)$.
    - Forward KL corresponds to maximum likelihood under an empirical data distribution.
      - $\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}} \Vert p_\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}[\log p_{\text{data}}(y \mid x)] - \mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}[\log p_\theta(y \mid x)]$.
      - The first term does not depend on $\theta$, so minimizing forward KL is equivalent to maximizing $\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}[\log p_\theta(y \mid x)]$.
      - With an empirical dataset, this is just supervised negative log-likelihood / cross-entropy on the dataset tokens.
  - Forward vs reverse KL under capacity mismatch:
    - Forward KL is mode-covering: the approximating distribution is pressured to put mass wherever $P$ has mass.
    - Reverse KL is mode-seeking: the approximating distribution can focus on one high-probability mode and avoid low-density regions.
    - For a limited-capacity student, this direction matters because it changes whether the student tries to cover all teacher behaviors or focus on a subset the teacher strongly supports.
  - Generalized JSD:
    - KL can be unbounded when one distribution gives zero probability to events the other distribution supports.
    - JSD compares each distribution to a mixture of the two, so it stays bounded even when supports are disjoint.
    - Generalized JSD uses $M_\beta = \beta P + (1-\beta)Q$:
      - $\mathrm{JSD}_\beta(P \Vert Q) = \beta \mathrm{KL}(P \Vert M_\beta) + (1-\beta)\mathrm{KL}(Q \Vert M_\beta)$.
    - $\beta$ interpolates between forward-KL-like and reverse-KL-like behavior.
    - Gradients behave more like forward KL when $\beta$ is near 0 and more like reverse KL when $\beta$ is near 1.
- **Section 3: Distillation for Autoregressive Sequence Models**:
  - Problem setup:
    - Teacher $\pi_T$ and student $\pi_S^\theta$ are autoregressive models with different capacity.
    - We have input prompts $X$ and optionally paired outputs $(X,Y)$.
    - For a given sequence $y$, define teacher/student discrepancy as the average token-level divergence over the prefixes in that sequence:
      - $\mathcal{D}(\pi_T \Vert \pi_S^\theta)(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{L_y}\sum_n \mathcal{D}(\pi_T(\cdot \mid y_{<n}, x) \Vert \pi_S^\theta(\cdot \mid y_{<n}, x))$.
    - Key point: the divergence is token-level, but evaluated along a full sequence/prefix path.
  - **Supervised FT**:
    - Used when we have a fixed dataset of ground-truth outputs and no teacher-query access.
    - Objective: maximize likelihood of dataset outputs under the student.
    - $L_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim(X,Y)}[-\log \pi_S^\theta(y \mid x)]$.
  - **Sequence-Level KD (SeqKD)**:
    - Same objective shape as SFT.
    - Difference: $y$ is generated by the teacher rather than taken from the human/ground-truth dataset.
    - Interpretable as SFT on teacher-generated outputs.
    - Uses hard teacher samples, not full token-level teacher distributions.
  - **Supervised KD**:
    - Train on a fixed dataset of sequences, but query the teacher for token-level distributions on those fixed prefixes.
    - Objective: $\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim(X,Y)}[\mathrm{KL}(\pi_T \Vert \pi_S^\theta)(y \mid x)]$.
    - Richer than SFT/SeqKD because the student sees the full teacher distribution over next tokens, not only the target token.
    - Still off-policy because prefixes come from a fixed dataset, not the current student.
  - Motivation for on-policy KD:
    - Fixed-prefix KD creates train-inference mismatch for autoregressive students.
    - At inference, the student conditions on its own generated prefixes.
    - If early tokens are wrong, future prefixes can leave the training distribution.
    - Analogy: behavior cloning trains on expert states; deployed learner visits learner-induced states.
  - **On-policy KD**:
    - Student generates $y \sim \pi_S(\cdot \mid x)$.
    - Teacher provides token-level probabilities on the student-generated prefixes.
    - Student is updated to imitate the teacher on states it actually visits.
    - Objective:
      - $L_{\mathrm{OD}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim X}\left[\mathbb{E}_{y\sim \pi_S(\cdot\mid x)}[\mathrm{KL}(\pi_T \Vert \pi_S^\theta)(y \mid x)]\right]$.
    - They do **not** backpropagate through the student's sampling distribution.
      - Sampled student outputs are treated as data.
      - The update is supervised-style, not a full policy-gradient update through discrete sampling.
      - This is why the method is stable and computationally efficient.
    - Student generation uses temperature $\gamma=1$ during training to encourage diversity.
    - Teacher still scores the student sequence, but does not need to autoregressively generate it; teacher-forced scoring is cheaper than teacher decoding.
  - **Generalized KD (GKD)**:
    - Unifies supervised KD and on-policy KD.
    - Two independent knobs:
      - Student data fraction $\lambda \in [0,1]$.
      - Divergence $\mathcal{D}$: forward KL, reverse KL, generalized JSD, etc.
    - Objective:
      - $(1-\lambda)$ times fixed-data distillation + $\lambda$ times on-policy student-generated distillation.
    - Special cases:
      - $\lambda=0$ + forward KL = supervised KD.
      - $\lambda=1$ + forward KL = on-policy KD.
      - $\lambda=1$ + reverse KL/JSD = on-policy GKD with mode-seeking-ish divergence.
    - Important: **on-policy vs off-policy** and **forward vs reverse KL** are orthogonal choices.
  - Practical assumption:
    - Student should already generate outputs of adequate quality for teacher feedback to be useful.
    - Experiments start from supervised-finetuned students.
    - This mirrors RLHF pipelines: SFT first, then online/on-policy optimization.
  - Divergence choice:
    - Forward KL pressures the student to cover the teacher's full token distribution.
    - With limited student capacity, this can put mass on tokens the teacher itself assigns low probability to, causing low-quality generations/hallucinations.
    - Reverse KL and high-$\beta$ JSD are more mode-seeking: prioritize teacher-high-probability tokens, usually improving quality but reducing diversity.
    - Optimal divergence is empirical/task-dependent.
  - **RL fine-tuning + on-policy GKD**:
    - They also propose adding GKD as a regularizer to RLHF/RLAIF-style optimization.
    - Objective trades off:
      - maximize scalar reward $r(y)$,
      - stay close to / distill from the teacher on student-generated sequences.
    - $\alpha$ controls distillation strength; $\alpha=1$ gives pure distillation.
    - Motivation: optimize reward while reducing alignment tax / preserving other capabilities via distillation.
    - If adding to an existing RLHF workflow, they recommend reverse KL or JSD$(0.9)$ because RLHF already commonly uses reverse-KL-style regularization.
- **Section 4: Experiments**:
  - Setup: T5-XL teacher (~3B) distilled into T5-small/base/large students on XSum, WMT14 en-de, GSM8K, and FLAN instruction-tuning.
  - Sweeps:
    - Data fraction $\lambda$: supervised/fixed ($0$), mixed ($0.5$), on-policy student samples ($1$).
    - Divergence: forward KL, reverse KL, JSD$(0.1/0.5/0.9)$.
  - Main result: **on-policy student-generated data is the robust win** across tasks; this is the core evidence for the train-inference mismatch story.
    - Overall comparison: Figure 1.
    - XSum baselines: Figure 2.
    - WMT ablation: Figure 6.
    - GSM8K ablations/data fraction: Figures 7-9.
  - Divergence is task/eval-dependent rather than universally fixed.
    - Figure 4: more reverse-KL-like objectives are more mode-seeking, trading diversity for sampled quality; intermediate JSD often gives a better tradeoff than pure reverse KL.
    - With greedy decoding, divergence differences shrink; with temperature sampling, the distribution shape matters more.
  - Data efficiency:
    - Figure 3: on-policy GKD with a small prompt subset can beat fixed-data baselines trained on the full XSum dataset.
  - RL + GKD:
    - Figure 5: combines RLAIF/RLEF factuality reward with on-policy GKD; $\alpha$ controls reward-vs-distillation tradeoff.
  - Task-agnostic instruction-tuning:
    - Figure `fig:flan_agnostic`: on-policy GKD with reverse KL improves held-out MMLU/BBH.

## [2025] [thinky] On-Policy Distillation

- **Date**: 2026-06-04
- **Blog**: <https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/on-policy-distillation/>

---

- **Core framing**:
  - SFT / off-policy distillation: off-policy data, dense token-level signal.
  - RL / RLVR: on-policy data, sparse scalar reward.
  - OPD: on-policy student rollouts, dense teacher token-level signal.
  - One-line version: student generates trajectories; teacher grades each token on the same student-generated prefixes.
- **Important differences vs Agarwal et al. / GKD**:
  - Agarwal et al. present a general framework:
    - data mixture $\lambda$: supervised/fixed vs mixed vs on-policy student samples.
    - divergence $\mathcal{D}$: forward KL, reverse KL, JSD, etc.
  - Thinking Machines chooses a narrower practical recipe:
    - fully on-policy student rollouts.
    - per-token reverse KL-like reward: $\log \pi_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t}) - \log \pi_T(x_t \mid x_{<t})$.
    - implemented through an RL-style importance-sampling loss by setting advantage to negative reverse KL.
  - Their OPD is closer to "dense reward RL" than supervised-style GKD:
    - student samples rollouts as in RL.
    - teacher logprobs become token-level rewards.
    - no separate reward model is needed.
  - Crucial assumption:
    - reverse-KL methods can only mode-seek within the student's support.
    - SFT/off-policy distillation or midtraining may be needed first to add support for relevant tokens/behaviors.
- **Reasoning experiment**:
  - Start with Qwen3-8B-Base and first do off-policy reasoning distillation on OpenThoughts-style teacher traces.
  - SFT gets the model into the right reasoning support; OPD then cheaply refines on student-generated reasoning trajectories.
  - Main claim: for reasoning, OPD can achieve the next increment of AIME performance much more cheaply than continuing SFT or running sparse-reward RL.
  - Qwen3 report anchor: OPD reaches stronger AIME/GPQA performance than RL at roughly one-tenth the GPU hours.
- **Personalization / continual learning experiment**:
  - Domain midtraining on internal documents improves knowledge but damages instruction-following behavior.
  - Mixing in chat data helps but does not fully recover IF-eval; LoRA learns less and still forgets.
  - OPD uses the earlier post-trained model as the teacher to recover instruction-following behavior after domain midtraining.
  - Key result: recover near-original IF-eval while preserving most of the new internal-document knowledge.
  - Interpretation: the old model acts as a dense behavior reward model for capabilities the adapted model forgot.
- **Discussion takeaways**:
  - **Dense supervision improves compute efficiency**:
    - RL gives roughly sequence-level sparse feedback.
    - OPD gives token-level feedback, i.e. many more bits per rollout.
    - Their direct RL-to-OPD experiment suggests OPD can recover an RL-trained policy in far fewer gradient steps.
    - Compute distinction:
      - RL must sample full rollouts and wait for sequence-level rewards; reward is often only meaningful after the answer is complete.
      - OPD can train on partial rollouts because every token has a teacher-logprob signal.
      - Teacher use is scoring, not generation: the smaller student autoregressively decodes the trajectory; the larger teacher runs teacher-forced `compute_logprobs` on that completed trajectory.
      - Teacher scoring is closer to a parallel prefill/forward pass over known tokens, whereas teacher generation would require expensive sequential decode steps.
      - This is why OPD can be cheaper than teacher-generated off-policy distillation when no fixed teacher dataset already exists.
    - Student / teacher cost asymmetry:
      - Sequential decoding is done by the cheaper student.
      - Expensive teacher compute is used for batched logprob evaluation.
      - The realized savings depend on serving implementation, batching, context length, KV cache, and GPU utilization; FLOP accounting can overstate teacher-logprob cost relative to wall-clock GPU hours.
    - Support caveat:
      - OPD is most efficient when the teacher behavior is already inside the student's support, e.g. after SFT/midtraining.
      - If the student cannot sample relevant reasoning/domain tokens, OPD needs larger batches or a prior SFT phase to add support.
  - **Prompt reuse / data efficiency**:
    - RL on repeated prompts can memorize final answers.
    - OPD trains toward the teacher distribution over trajectories, so many sampled completions from the same prompt can still provide useful signal.
  - **RL searches; distillation compresses**:
    - RL spends compute exploring semantic strategies through rollouts and credit assignment.
    - Once RL finds a good final policy/strategy, OPD can transfer that behavior without replaying the whole RL curriculum.
  - **On-policy learning for continual learning**:
    - SFT on fixed samples can become off-policy after the model updates, causing drift and forgetting.
    - OPD keeps collecting rollouts from the current student while the teacher stays fixed, so the correction remains targeted to the student's current behavior.
    - Practical loop: learn new knowledge with SFT/midtraining, then use OPD from an earlier/stronger teacher to recover behavior.
- **Caveats**:
  - Teacher must provide useful logprobs on student-visited prefixes.
  - Student must already have enough support for the teacher behavior; otherwise OPD needs SFT/midtraining first.
  - Reverse KL can over-concentrate behavior and reduce diversity.
  - Compute comparisons are implementation-dependent because teacher scoring, student sampling, and GPU utilization have different costs.
